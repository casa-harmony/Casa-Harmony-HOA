01

What an HOA is, in plain language

If the domain is new to you, this is the whole thing in six points. Everything the software does exists to serve one of them.

  • In America, buying a home in a planned community — a condo tower, a gated development, a townhouse row — automatically makes you a member of a Homeowners Association. It is a small non-profit corporation that owns the shared property: roads, pool, gym, lobby, roof, landscaping, gates.
  • Every homeowner pays a monthly fee, called dues or assessments — typically $200 to $700. This is not rent and not tax. It is the community's own money.
  • That money pays the shared bills: pool service, elevator contracts, insurance, landscaping, security, trash removal.
  • Homeowners elect a Board of Directors from among themselves. They are unpaid volunteer neighbours who approve spending and set policy. Because volunteers cannot run a business day to day, the Board hires a property management company, whose managers and accountants do the actual work.
  • The community has a rulebook. Break it and you receive a violation notice, then a fine. Fail to pay your dues and an escalating ladder begins: reminder letters, then a payment plan, then collections, and ultimately a lien — a legal claim that blocks the sale of your home until the debt clears.
  • The money sits in two separate pots. The Operating Fund pays this month's bills. The Reserve Fund is long-term savings — the roof will need replacing in twelve years and will cost $400,000, so a little is set aside every month. Most American states legally require these funds to be kept and reported separately.
Why this shapes the software

That last point is the reason the accounting here is fund accounting rather than ordinary business accounting. Every single transaction in the system — every purchase order line, every invoice distribution, every journal line — must declare which fund it belongs to. The system enforces it; you cannot post money without saying which pot it came from.

What Casa Harmony is

One platform with two halves that feed each other.

The Service Desk half is where problems arrive. A resident reports a leak, a complaint, a rule violation, or a request. Staff triage it, assign it, and drive it to closed.

The ERP half is the accounting back office: chart of accounts, budgets, purchase orders, vendor bills, homeowner invoices, payments, bank reconciliation, fixed assets, financial reporting, and month-end close.

The ERP half is modelled deliberately on Oracle E-Business Suite. That is why the vocabulary is what it is — key flexfields, value sets, code combinations, subledgers, encumbrances, three-way matching, dunning, distribution sets. None of it is invented; it is standard enterprise finance terminology, and it is the reason this can be sold to management companies who already run real accounting departments.

A naming trap, flagged early

In this codebase the word “tenant” means one HOA — it is the multi-tenancy sense of the word, not a renter. A renter is a resident whose type is RENTER. Every screen labelled “Tenants” is a list of HOAs. This causes confusion in every conversation, and the client-facing labels should say “Communities” or “HOAs” instead.

Two identity systems, not one

This is the single most important architectural fact about access in this product.

Staff identity — global person, per-community role

A staff member has one login for the whole platform. Their access to any particular community is a separate grant called a membership, which is the triple (person, community, role). One person can hold different roles in different communities:

PersonCommunityRole thereWhat that means
jane@pm-co.com
one password		Sunnyvale HOAHOA_ADMINFull control — she manages this community
Oakridge HOAACCOUNTANTBooks only — someone else manages it
Pinecrest HOAVIEWERRead-only — she is just covering

Jane signs in once and switches communities from a dropdown in the header. Her powers change as she switches. This mirrors exactly how a property management company operates — one manager covering several communities at different levels of responsibility.

Resident identity — scoped to a community, then to units

A resident is a completely separate kind of account. Their username is unique within one community, not across the platform. One resident can be linked to many units — a landlord owning three condos has one login and sees three units. Co-owners of a single unit each get their own login. Each unit is an accounts-receivable account in the ledger, so what a resident sees on screen is a direct view of their own financial record.

Residents get multi-factor authentication by one-time code over email or SMS, not authenticator apps — a deliberate decision, since residents are not technical users. Staff get standard authenticator-app codes.

04

Role explorer — what each person sees and can do

Select a role. The screens listed are exactly what appears in their sidebar; the greyed and struck-through ones are hidden from them. Every capability is a real permission code checked by the server on every request.

Two access gaps to settle now

The Viewer role is too narrow to give a board member. As built, VIEWER can see the chart of accounts and nothing else — not the budget, not the financial reports, not the board dashboard. A real board member needs to read all of those and change none of them. The code already refers to a BOARD_MEMBER role when it sends out the monthly board packet, but that role was never defined. It should be.

The current screens do not enforce roles. The server refuses unauthorised requests correctly, but the sidebar shows almost every screen to everybody — only the community-switching screen is hidden. So an accountant currently sees menu items that will reject them. The sidebar needs to be driven by the same permission list the server uses.

05

How one community's data is kept from another

The platform runs one system serving many communities at once. Separation is enforced twice, independently, so that a mistake in one layer cannot leak data on its own.

What else is protected

  • Encryption inside the database. Sensitive columns — resident phone numbers, authentication secrets — are encrypted at rest, so they are unreadable even to someone holding a copy of the database file.
  • Card details are never stored. Payment cards are exchanged for a token held by the payment provider. The system stores the token, never the number.
  • Every change is logged. An append-only audit trail records who changed what and when. It cannot be edited after the fact.
  • Privacy requests are supported. A resident can request a copy of their data, an export, or deletion — the flows required by Californian privacy law are built in.
  • Forced password change. Any account created by an administrator must set a new password before it can do anything else.

The operating cycle — the whole product in one picture

Money comes in from homeowners on the left. Work goes out to vendors on the right. Everything meets in the ledger at the bottom, and the ledger feeds the board's view at the top. Every screen in the application is one station on this loop.

The three-way match, explained

This is the control that stops a community paying for work it never received. Before an invoice can be paid, three independent documents must agree:

  • The purchase order — what we agreed to buy, and for how much.
  • The receipt — what actually arrived, confirmed by a person who saw it.
  • The invoice — what the vendor is asking to be paid.

Each community sets its own tolerance — how far the invoice may drift from the order before the system objects, expressed as a percentage on amount and quantity, and whether a receipt is required at all. Inside tolerance the invoice matches automatically. Outside it, the invoice is flagged and placed on hold, and no payment can be issued until a person releases it and records why.

What separation of duties looks like here

Notice that the accountant can raise a purchase order and enter an invoice, but cannot approve either. Approval requires po.approve and ap.approve, which the accountant role does not hold. The person who prepares a payment is structurally not the person who authorises it. This is deliberate and is the kind of control an auditor will look for.

How approvals work

Approvals are configured, not hard-coded. Each community defines its own rules, and the amount of the document decides how many people must sign.

The rule that drives everything

A community sets up an approval hierarchy per document type — purchase orders, vendor invoices, contracts, journal batches, work orders. Each hierarchy contains numbered levels, and each level says: from this amount upward, this role must approve.

When a document is submitted, the system counts how many levels apply to that amount. A $400 purchase order might need one signature; a $40,000 one might need three. The document then moves up the levels one at a time.

Signatures needed123

Illustrative thresholds — each community configures its own on the Approvals screen.

Points worth confirming with the client

  • Rejection is total, not partial. One decline ends the request. There is no “send back to level 1” — the author fixes the document and resubmits from the start.
  • No hierarchy means no approval. If a community has not configured a hierarchy for a document type, documents of that type approve automatically. This is convenient for small communities and dangerous for large ones. It should probably be a setup-checklist warning.
  • Levels are assigned to roles, not people. Anyone holding the approver role at that level can sign. There is currently no named-individual approver, no delegation, and no out-of-office substitute.
  • The full history is kept. Every action, approval or rejection, is stored with the approver, the level, the time, and the comment.
10

Everything lands in the ledger

The general ledger is where the product ends. Nothing flows out of it into the operational modules — it is the destination, and the reports are drawn from it.

How an account code is built

Rather than a fixed account number, the system uses a configurable code made of segments. A default setup has three, and up to fifteen are supported:

SegmentExampleMeansWhy it exists
Fund10Operating FundMandatory on every posting — the legal separation of the two pots
Department200MaintenanceWhich part of the community spent it
Account5000SuppliesWhat kind of money it is

Combined, 10-200-5000 means Operating Fund, Maintenance department, Supplies expense. Each segment draws its allowed values from a value set, and only combinations that pass validation rules can be used — which is what stops someone charging a reserve-fund expense to an operating account by accident.

The posting chain and the month-end gate

StageWhat happensWho
Subledger activityInvoices, receipts, payments and receipts of goods accumulate in the receivables and payables ledgers.Everyday users
Journal batch createdThose transactions are gathered into a balanced batch of journal entries. Debits must equal credits or it cannot proceed.Accountant
Batch approvedA second person reviews and approves the batch.Accountant / HOA Admin
PostedThe batch hits the ledger. Balances move. This is irreversible — corrections are made with a new reversing entry, never by editing.Accountant
Period closedThe month is locked. Nothing further can post to it, so the reported numbers cannot change after the fact.Accountant / HOA Admin
Year-end closeThe year's results are rolled forward into the opening balances of the next year.Accountant / HOA Admin
Why period close matters to the client

Once a month is closed, its numbers are frozen. That is what lets a board present financial statements and an auditor rely on them — nobody can quietly change last quarter's figures. It also means the close must be run deliberately every month, and that a mistake found after closing is corrected by posting a new entry in the current month, not by rewriting history.

What the board actually sees

  • Executive dashboard — cash by fund, outstanding receivables, delinquency totals, open cases, active payment plans, liens filed, and an ageing breakdown by fund.
  • Cash-flow forecast — projected opening balance, inflows, outflows and closing balance per month per fund.
  • Budget versus actual — every account line with its budget, its actual, and the variance.
  • Reserve study versus actual — whether the community is saving enough against the professional replacement schedule.
  • The monthly board packet — assembled and delivered automatically on a schedule.

Which flows are one-way, and which travel both ways

This is worth being precise about, because it determines what can be corrected later and what cannot. Most document flows travel forward; a smaller set genuinely loops back, and the loops are where the business logic lives.

BetweenDirectionWhat travels back, if anything
Ticket → Purchase order Both ways The ticket creates the PO going forward, and the PO's identifier is written back onto the ticket — so the ticket screen can show the spend and its approval status without leaving the page.
PO → Receipt → Invoice Both ways Documents are created forward, but matching reads backward — the invoice is validated against the PO and receipt. The amount billed is then written back onto the PO, so the PO always knows how much of its limit is consumed.
PO → Encumbrance Forward then reversed Approving a PO reserves the money. Invoicing against it releases the reservation. A deliberate matched pair — a reservation that is never released would permanently overstate committed spend.
Document → Approval Both ways Submission goes forward through the levels; approval returns permission to act, and rejection returns the document to its author with the reason.
Budget ↔ Transactions A closed loop The budget constrains new spending going forward; posted actuals feed back into it. In advisory mode an overspend warns; in absolute mode it blocks. This is the only true two-way control loop in the system.
Invoice → Dunning → Collections Both ways Escalation moves forward automatically by days past due; payment moves the case back to good standing from any stage.
Subledgers → General ledger One way only Nothing. Receivables and payables post into the ledger and the ledger never pushes back. It is the endpoint. Corrections are new entries, never edits.
Posting → Period close One way only Nothing. Closing is a gate that shuts. Reopening a closed period is an administrative act with an audit trail, not a normal operation.
Everything → Audit log One way only Nothing. The audit trail is append-only by design. Nothing reads back out of it into the business flow — it exists to be read by people.
Payment → Bank reconciliation Both ways Payments go out and appear on the bank statement; reconciliation matches them and marks the payment cleared, writing status back onto the payment record.
Staff → Resident portal Both ways Staff actions surface to the resident as invoices, statements and documents; the resident's payments and requests come back into the staff ledger and service desk.
Staff → Vendor Currently neither Nothing at all — vendors have no account. This is the gap in section 12, and the client should decide which way it needs to travel.
The rule underneath all of this

Operational documents can be corrected; accounting records cannot. A ticket can be reassigned, a purchase order revised before approval, an invoice held and released. But once something posts to the ledger it is permanent, and once a period closes it is frozen. Every correction after that point is a new, dated, attributed entry. That distinction is what makes the financial statements defensible.

12

Notifications — how work reaches the right inbox

The mechanism for “something happened, tell the right person” is built and working for staff. It does not reach vendors, and that limitation is structural rather than a missing feature.

How it works today

A notification can be addressed in three ways, and this flexibility is the useful part:

  • To one named person — “ticket SRV-1042 has been assigned to you”.
  • To everyone holding a role — “the monthly board packet is ready” goes to every board member without naming them.
  • To everyone in the community — a general announcement.

Each notification carries a category, a message, a read/unread flag, and a link back to the thing it is about — so clicking it opens the invoice, ticket or batch in question. Alongside the in-app inbox there is an outbound channel for email and text messages.

Why “assign a ticket to a vendor and it appears in their inbox” does not work yet

A ticket can already carry a vendor and an estimated cost, and can be converted into a purchase order for that vendor. That part is real. But a notification is addressed to a user account, and a vendor is not a user — a vendor is a supplier record with no login, no password, and no portal. There is no inbox to deliver to.

This is not a bug to fix; it is a product decision to make. The three options are laid out in section 16.

Documents and file uploads

The storage model is already built and is more flexible than most. What is missing is the screen to use it.

How attachments work

A file is not attached to a single fixed place. Each stored file records what kind of thing it belongs to and which one — which means the same mechanism serves every module without any new code:

Attach a file toTypical useWho can see it
A service ticketPhoto of the leak, the vendor's quoteStaff; the reporting resident if linked
A vendor invoiceScan of the billStaff with document access
A purchase order or contractThe signed contract documentStaff with document access
A fixed assetWarranty, inspection reportStaff with document access
A board meetingMinutes, agenda, financial packStaff; board members
The community itselfRules, bylaws, insurance certificateStaff; residents, if published
A specific homeownerTheir statement, their violation noticeStaff; that one resident only

Files are stored under an opaque key rather than their original filename, and every download is permission-checked — so a file cannot be reached by guessing a URL. Each record keeps the original filename, its type, its size, who uploaded it, and optional notes.

What still needs building

The storage and the permissions exist. The interface does not: there is no drag-and-drop area, no file picker, no upload progress, no preview, and no attachments panel on the ticket, invoice or purchase-order screens. This is front-end work only — the demo can show a fully convincing upload experience running entirely in the browser, and connecting it to the real storage afterwards is a small change in one place.

Three things to fix before the demo is shown

Nothing can be saved. The demo returns the same fixed data regardless of what you do, so every Create, Save, Approve and Upload button opens, accepts input, and then changes nothing. A client who tries to add a ticket will notice immediately. Making the demo remember changes in the browser is a small piece of work and fixes every button at once.

Half the screens are empty. Sample data is needed for the sixteen listed above.

The resident portal does not open. It bypasses the demo data layer and tries to reach a live server, so it fails and returns to its login screen. If the client asks what a homeowner sees — a reasonable question — there is currently nothing to show.

None of the demo work is wasted

The demo build is a copy of the real application, differing only in where it gets its data. Every improvement made to the demo — a new screen, a better table, the upload interface, the ticket timeline — carries directly across to the live product. Only the data layer is swapped back.

16

Decisions needed before we build further

Six questions. Each one changes what gets built, and answering them now is far cheaper than answering them later.

Decision 1 — how do vendors participate?

This is the largest question and it changes the scope materially.

OptionWhat the vendor getsWhat it costsFits when
A. Email only An email when a job is assigned, with a link to a single read-only page showing the work order. Small Vendors are small local contractors who will never log into a system.
B. A vendor portal Their own login and portal — see assigned jobs, accept or decline, update progress, upload photos and quotes, submit invoices. Large Vendors are recurring contractors and the client wants to cut out phone and email chasing.
C. Internal only Nothing. “Assign to vendor” is a record; the inbox notification goes to the staff member who owns the ticket. Mostly built Staff will always be the ones contacting vendors directly.

Recommendation: build option C now, since it is close to working already, and mock up option B as a screen in the demo purely to gauge the client's reaction. Option B is a third identity system alongside staff and residents — it should be quoted and scheduled separately, not absorbed into the current scope.

Decision 2 — should board members get a proper role?

Today the only read-only role can see the chart of accounts and nothing else. A board member realistically needs to read the budget, the financial reports, the board dashboard, open tickets and community documents — and change none of it. Confirm that scope and it becomes a small addition.

Decision 3 — what should the approval thresholds be?

The engine is fully configurable, but somebody has to supply the actual numbers: at what amount does a purchase order need the treasurer, and at what amount the full board? The client's existing spending policy is the answer. Also confirm whether a document with no configured hierarchy should approve automatically, as it does now, or be blocked until rules are set.

Decision 4 — how strict should budget control be?

Two modes exist. Advisory warns when a purchase would exceed the budget but lets it through. Absolute blocks it outright. Advisory is the safer starting point; the client should confirm which they expect, since absolute mode will stop people working when a budget line runs out.

Decision 5 — what are the matching tolerances?

How far may a vendor's invoice exceed the agreed order before it is held for review — a percentage, a flat amount, or zero? And must a receipt always be recorded before payment, or only for physical goods? These numbers directly determine how much manual review the accounting team faces every month.

Decision 6 — language on screen

The interface currently uses the internal vocabulary in places — most importantly “Tenants” where it means communities. Confirm the wording the client's own staff use, and the labels should follow it rather than the database.

What happens once these are settled

  • First — make the demo behave like real software: let it remember changes, fill the empty screens, and repair the resident portal.
  • Then — build the story the client watches: switching between roles to see the application change, the ticket timeline with comments and vendor assignment, the notification inbox that updates live, and real file uploads.
  • Then — visual polish, permission-aware navigation, and the board member role.
  • Finally, on approval — connect the live application to the server module by module, reusing every screen built for the demo.
